How it works

A tile joins its six edges in three pairs, drawn as a corridor through the hexagon. Corridors always meet across shared edges, so the figure is a set of closed loops, and each one gets its own color. Flipping a tile changes the loop count by −2 if its three corridors lie on three different loops (they merge), 0 if on two (they rearrange), or +2 if on one (it splits) — never an odd number, which is why a single loop is hard to reach: you can never merge just two.

Color follows the loop, not its size, so anything a flip does not touch keeps its color. Rhombi fills each loop's territory instead of drawing its curve: a tile cuts its hexagon into three rhombi, one per corner it wraps, and each belongs to exactly one loop. Show outer path brings back whatever runs out past the tiles, which is hidden by default; the count is always of the paths you can see. Lock border pins the outer ring so its walls close into a band, turning the figure into an enclosed maze; with it on, an odd radius can be clicked down to a single enclosed corridor, while an even one bottoms out at two.

Solve plays it out. Where a cell touches three loops it merges there, choosing the merge that leaves the remaining loops closest together; where none does, it searches for the shortest set of pushes that opens one — exact within a fixed budget. Past that it follows the spread — the distance between the loops — and the status says so, since that move is chosen rather than proven. It never splits a loop, because it never needs to. It stops as soon as fewer than three loops are reachable, which is a proof that no merge remains rather than a timeout. Expect long stretches of plain merging and a visible struggle at the end — bringing the last three loops together is the only hard step.
